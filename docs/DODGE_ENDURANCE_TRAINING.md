# Dodge endurance training

The isolated Dodge player aims to reach a displayed score of 50 while the player is still alive. In the local Godot demo, the score is incremented by a one-second game timer; it is not a count of enemies evaded. This is a visual gameplay test in a private WSL display, with no control of the Windows desktop. The policy observes rendered frames and selects bounded movement inputs; it does not receive the game's internal player or enemy positions.

The test-only game evaluator records the score and whether the player is alive. It is used to stop and verify the score goal, not to choose actions. A separate audit file can record native enemy and player positions for offline tracking diagnostics; neither the policy nor the goal supervisor reads that audit. A successful run saves `score-goal.bmp` alongside the report. Endurance runs save every tenth action's before/after frames and all uncertain or terminal frames, plus decision and action records. New runs are limited to 1,200 wall-clock seconds, 2,400 actions and 9,600 requests; reaching a limit is not success. Earlier reports used 690 seconds, 1,200 actions and 4,800 requests. Reports and comparisons keep these profiles distinct. The private game and display are stopped after a run.

At 25% stepped speed, `center-first-v12`, pack version `0.2.2`, seed 118 reached score **50**, with the player still visible, after 301 movement actions. The independent evaluator reported the score and alive status, and the saved frame visibly shows both the score and the player. This is one successful controlled scenario, not a demonstrated success rate, a full-speed result, or a claim that 50 enemies were avoided. Earlier scenarios for the same policy ended at scores 6 and 13; earlier policies also had failures. The local evidence is `.local/wsl-dodge-runs/wsl-dodge-4747c2bcf3c1466f9e0eff6a2fac14c6/`.

At 50% stepped speed, the same policy and seed reached score 16 before game over. At 100% stepped speed it reached score 50 while alive, after 296 actions; the saved frame and report are in `.local/wsl-dodge-runs/wsl-dodge-e57061a6a4f34e99b0a75873b2d49427/`. Different simulation granularities can produce different trajectories, so these are additional scenarios rather than controlled tests of speed alone.

An additional 25% run on seed 781 ended at score 13. Its offline audit showed the player close to the left boundary and an enemy entering from that boundary shortly before game over. The visual tracker identified that enemy only near the edge and did not yet know its velocity. This suggests an incoming enemy can become dangerous before its full body is visible; the audit does not by itself establish the exact cause of the collision. The native audit is evidence for improving visual recognition and boundary handling, not an input to the player.

Stepped tests pause between observations and inputs, even at the 100% setting. They are useful for practicing and comparing simulation granularity but do not measure real-time reaction latency. Do not advance to real-time tests until the slow-stage performance is repeatable across complete varied games.

In Local test games, select a stepped speed and **Train toward score 50** to run this optional benchmark. It defaults to the experimental center-first pack when no other pack is selected. The displayed stop reason distinguishes reaching the goal from game over, cancelled, limits or uncertain outcomes.

## Complete-game average

Reaching 50 once is not the benchmark. `--benchmark-wsl-dodge` continues each bounded game until visual game-over recognition, and records its final score using the test-only evaluator. `tools/run_dodge_benchmark.py` runs a predeclared seed list sequentially; `tools/benchmark_dodge_scores.py` reports an average only if **every** scheduled game completed. Missing games, limits, cancellation, recognition errors, and earlier tests stopped at 50 remain incomplete. Reports must use the same policy, pack, mode, speed and budgets; the runner fingerprints the game, controller, pack and bridge files before and after each run.

The first development baseline used the four known scenarios in `tools/fixtures/dodge-benchmark-development.json` at 25% stepped speed. Their completed scores were **92, 6, 13 and 21**, for a mean of **33** and a median of **17**. This is below the requested mean of 50; three out of four games scored below 50. The audited summary is `.local/dodge-benchmarks/3b12e750b9a74d759e604bc58bda339d/summary-verified-v2.json`. The development scenarios were already used during policy design and do not establish generalization. The original predeclared ten-seed validation list has since been used; future studies reserve fresh, disjoint seeds. Ten runs remain a limited sample.

After the score-overlay recognition changes, a fresh complete four-game run of the same center-first policy and development openings scored **66, 6, 13 and 21**, for a mean of **26.5** and a median of **17**. The report is `.local/dodge-benchmarks/cbdd1539cae24b59aa49c92114871f38/summary.json`. Recognition changed between the older and newer sets, so the different score for the first opening cannot be attributed solely to randomness. Neither set demonstrates repeatability or meets the requested goal.

The benchmark currently covers private, slowed, stepped games only. An average of 50 there is an intermediate training target, not proof of continuous real-time play or of Windows-desktop performance. Move to those tests only after the slow-stage fundamentals and varied validation runs are dependable.

The first adaptive-center candidate completed the same four scenarios at 28, 6, 2 and 21 (mean **14.25**); a trajectory candidate scored 4, 4, 7 and 15 (mean **7.5**). Both were worse than the development baseline and remain experimental. A subsequent predictive-tracking candidate completed three games at 71, 2 and 21, but its fourth stopped at 28 with the player still alive and visually obscured by the score; consequently there is **no four-game mean** for that trial. Saved frames from that failure have been added to recognition regression tests. These development results do not use the reserved validation scenarios.

After correcting the score-overlay tracking, the predictive-tracking candidate completed all four scenarios at 72, 30, 2 and 21, with a mean of **31.25**; it was not promoted over the baseline. The comparison includes both tracking and score-overlay recognition changes, so it cannot isolate the effect of either. Another candidate reuses each observed post-action frame as the next decision frame, eliminating an unnecessary no-input simulation step between consecutive moves. Its cadence differs from earlier runs and is recorded explicitly in every report; it still pauses the stepped game while deciding.

The first cadence candidate completed the four development scenarios at **28, 11, 14 and 5**, averaging **14.5**. Its control loop removes the artificial idle gap, but the existing avoidance policy did not perform well under that timing. It is not the default; compare policies under the same cadence before drawing conclusions about the policy alone.

Combining that cadence with predictive tracking scored **28, 41, 14 and 5**, averaging **22** with a median of **21**. It improved one scenario compared with the cadence-only version but did not reach the mean of 50 or outperform the 33-point baseline overall. All of these are development comparisons; validation and real-time play remain outstanding.

A configurable longer prediction horizon scored **16, 11, 92 and 5** on those same development scenarios, averaging **31**. An adaptive-horizon policy scored **81, 6, 13 and 21**, averaging **30.25**. A more expensive three-segment forecast scored **7 and 2** in two focused difficult scenarios and was not selected for a complete benchmark. None of these results meets the goal or justifies increasing the training speed.

The experimental velocity guard rejects a sudden large change in an enemy's estimated velocity instead of treating it as certain. In two focused difficult openings it scored **13 and 9**, compared with **13 and 6** for the latest center-first baseline. That small focused comparison is not a four-game average or evidence of generalization; the guard has not been promoted as a successful player.

A separate wall-aware fallback scored **42 and 5** in those openings, compared with the baseline's **13 and 6**. Its fallback adds a penalty for ending a step close to a boundary; it still uses visual enemy observations and chooses a fresh action at each step. The subsequent full development comparison scored **28, 5, 42 and 3**, averaging **19.5**, so it was not promoted over the 26.5 baseline. An edge preference can also move the player toward a threat; both immediate clearance and room to maneuver need evaluation.

Adding an immediate-clearance guard to the wall-aware fallback scored **13 and 6** in the same two focused openings. It therefore did not retain the improvement in the first scenario and was not promoted. Both focused results are in `.local/dodge-benchmarks/e778fb20d1f241d1a8db9f02b941c6f6/summary.json`.

For offline perception and control diagnostics, `python tools/analyze_dodge_tracking.py <run-directory>` compares recorded action-frame observations with that isolated game's audit. The game audit is never supplied to the policy. The analyzer aligns captured times with stepped ticks, checks movement in the requested direction and uses approximate one-to-one proximity matches; it reports unmatched samples and errors where velocity was asserted. A small movement can also result from a boundary or collision, so it is not by itself proof of an input failure. The tool does not inspect every frame, establish object identity, measure real-time accuracy or prove why a collision occurred. Score-overlay recognition also now withholds advice if multiple possible player landmarks are present, rather than choosing a position from ambiguous evidence. A run stopped for uncertain recognition remains incomplete, not a successful game.

The user-facing goal is repeatable **mean final score of at least 50 across games**, including low-scoring games rather than stopping each game at 50. First improve the slowed development results and inspect the distribution, then evaluate the chosen candidate on the untouched validation manifest. A passing slowed validation is only a prerequisite for continuous real-world testing, not proof of it. Keep tests private, sequential and resource-bounded; do not increase speed merely because one run reaches 50.

## Offline parameter learning

`tools/train_dodge_offline.py` automates a small search over a rule-based player's forecast horizon and threat radius. It creates immutable candidate packs, runs the baseline and each candidate through complete games in the private display, and ranks results only after all declared games finish with the same observation cadence. New studies select candidates using a screening mean capped at twice the target per game, with lower scores retained, to reduce the influence of a single long run. This cap is a selection heuristic only: development and validation still report and qualify against the actual uncapped mean. This is score-based parameter search, not a trained neural network. Game state from the private audit is not supplied to the player.

This local game search interacts with a running game. For the separate machine-learning workflow that trains only from saved recordings, see [Dodge offline learning](DODGE_OFFLINE_LEARNING.md).

The experimental `--policy directional-v26` searches a different heuristic: it predicts what happens if a direction is repeated, including the stepped loop's key-release interval, and chooses a fresh bounded movement after the next image. This model of future motion is approximate and applies only to this isolated stepped workflow; it must earn its place through the same complete-game comparisons.

The experimental `directional-configurable-v30` keeps that visual planner and exposes bounded risk and edge weights in the game pack. The offline search can compare a small grid of these settings through `--risk-weights` and `--edge-weights`. Equal weights of 100 reproduce the earlier planner's scoring; other settings are hypotheses, not established improvements. Each selected setting is regenerated and checked byte-for-byte before the full development stage.

The experimental `directional-two-phase-v31` considers a possible turn partway through its forecast, then executes only one short move and reobserves. It is not a promise that a later turn will remain safe; it must be evaluated against complete varied games like every other candidate.

The fallback requires a unique landmark and nearby player-colored pixels; unseen cases may still be ambiguous and stop safely. The default **focus** study uses two difficult development openings and can identify a candidate for further investigation; its mean is not evidence of performance across varied games. The **development** study uses all four development openings. The separate ten-seed validation manifest is never accessed by this training tool, and the tool does not install or promote a candidate. Even a passing development score requires separate held-out validation and, later, continuous real-world testing.

For an isolated study, run:

```powershell
python tools/train_dodge_offline.py . ProjectMaelstrom/ProjectMaelstrom/bin/Debug/net9.0-windows10.0.22621.0/ProjectMaelstrom.exe tools/fixtures/game_packs/dodge-context-v12/pack.json --scope focus --minutes 45 --horizons 700 --radii 50
```

The tool accepts at most four candidate combinations and a budget from 25 to 180 minutes. A game starts only if its full 1,245-second process timeout fits in the remaining budget. It runs one game at a time and uses an OS-released exclusive study lock, immutable pack versions and fingerprints of core game scripts, scenes, visual assets and controller files. These fingerprints do not cover the entire operating system or runtime. It writes a `study.json` checkpoint and game reports under `.local/dodge-training/`; creating a file named `STOP` in that study's folder prevents starting the next game. An interrupted or incomplete game leaves the study incomplete; it is never silently excluded or replaced. This is currently a developer workflow, not a finished in-app training screen.

`tools/run_dodge_curriculum.py` can chain a focused parameter search, a complete development comparison for the selected candidate, and one held-out validation run when the development mean reaches 50. It never uses validation results to tune, installs nothing, and does not start real-time play. It reserves enough remaining time for all ten validation games at their maximum limits before touching the held-out list. If time is insufficient, it stops with `budget_before_validation` instead of running a partial held-out set. The curriculum has an explicit 45–300 minute total budget and runs only one game at a time. Creating `STOP` in the printed curriculum folder stops the curriculum after its current game, including at stage boundaries; a `STOP` in an active study folder also prevents the next stage. This is still a command-line development tool. A slow validation result would still need repeatability and real-time qualification.

For a previously completed focused study, `--from-focus <study-directory>` rechecks the declared packs, report files, game-over evidence, score summaries and selection before using it to choose a candidate. This only avoids repeating the preliminary screen. It runs a fresh full development comparison with matching current files and limits; it does not combine old and new scores.

Alternatively, `--baseline-from <benchmark-directory>` reuses only the preliminary focus baseline after rechecking the manifest, game and executable fingerprints, pack hash, score reports and saved summary. It copies the verified summary into the new study and records its origin. It is rejected for full development; those games and all held-out validation remain fresh. The source reports and screenshots must remain available for later verification.

If a qualifying curriculum stops at `budget_before_validation`, `tools/resume_dodge_validation.py` can run its reserved holdout later. It rechecks every development report and the original file fingerprints, requires a full bounded validation budget, and refuses to retry a holdout that has already started. It cannot promote a pack or start real-time play.
